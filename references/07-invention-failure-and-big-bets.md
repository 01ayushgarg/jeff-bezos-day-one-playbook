# 07 · Invention, failure and big bets

> "Failure comes part and parcel with invention. It's not optional." [L2013]

## What he said

**Bold, from the start (1997).** One of the 1997 principles: "We will make bold rather than timid investment
decisions where we see a sufficient probability of gaining market leadership advantages." [L1997] Some will
pay off and some won't, and either way Amazon learns (our paraphrase [L1997]).

**Fail early, double down (2013).** Amazon believes in failing early and iterating; most experiments can start
small, and when something works for customers, it doubles down (our paraphrase [L2013]). He adds that it won't
always be that clean: over time Amazon will also fail at some big bets (our paraphrase [L2013]).

**The math of big bets (2015).** "failure and invention are inseparable twins. To invent you have to
experiment, and if you know in advance that it's going to work, it's not an experiment." [L2015] Then the
arithmetic: "Given a ten percent chance of a 100 times payoff, you should take that bet every time. But you're
still going to be wrong nine times out of ten." [L2015] Baseball caps a swing at four runs; business doesn't:
"every once in a while, when you step up to the plate, you can score 1,000 runs." [L2015] So: "Big winners pay
for so many experiments." [L2015] He said it again in 2018, with a bigger number: "the big winners pay for
thousands of failed experiments." [ECW18]

**His own misses (2014, 2015, 2018).** Marketplace came after two failures, Auctions and zShops. Of Auctions:
"I think seven people came, if you count my parents and siblings." [L2014] In 2015: "We learned from our
failures and stayed stubborn on the vision" [L2015]. The Fire phone failed, but its lessons and its developers
went into building Alexa (our paraphrase [L2018]).

**Failure has to scale (2018).** "If the size of your failures isn't growing, you're not going to be inventing
at a size that can actually move the needle." [L2018] For Amazon that meant occasional multibillion-dollar
failures, taken as good bets and not lightly (our paraphrase [L2018]).

**Wandering (2018, 2023).** "Wandering is an essential counter-balance to efficiency." [L2018] When you know
where you're going, plan and execute; but "The outsized discoveries – the 'non-linear' ones – are highly likely
to require wandering." [L2018] In 2023: "Efficiency and invention are sort of at odds" [LEX405 00:22:26], and
"you have to give yourself permission to wander." [LEX405 00:22:26] AWS and the voice assistant are his examples
of things no customer asked for (our paraphrase [L2018]).

**Protect new ideas (2011, 2023).** On self-service platforms: "even well-meaning gatekeepers slow innovation."
[L2011] On new ideas inside a team, he warns colleagues up front: "it is going to be really easy for you to find
objections to this idea, but work with me." [LEX405 00:24:31] New ideas are easy to kill early, because the
easy objections come first (our paraphrase [LEX405 00:24:54]).

**Blind alleys (2012).** "Our passion for pioneering will drive us to explore narrow passages, and, unavoidably,
many will turn out to be blind alleys." [L2012]

**The start-up version (2001).** For a tiny company the advice is different: with early, scarce money, "you go
about systematically trying to eliminate risk." [AOA01] Pick the biggest problem and remove it, then the next
(our paraphrase [AOA01]).

**Our reading of the evolution:** in 2001 he is a start-up founder removing risk one piece at a time. By 2015
and 2018 he is running one of the largest companies in the world and deliberately adding risk, because size
lets it absorb big failures. Know which situation you're in.

## How to apply it: the experiment portfolio (our suggestion)

1. **List every bet** in progress: experiments, new products, new markets.
2. **Size each.** Cost to try, odds of working (honest guess), payoff if it works. Mark long-tail bets: small
   odds, very large payoff [L2015].
3. **Mark the door.** Is a failure reversible? (chapter 05). Start reversible bets small [L2013].
4. **Set the failure budget.** A share of time and money you expect to lose on experiments this year. **Our
   suggestion:** make it explicit, and check it grows as the company grows [L2018].
5. **Write the double-down signal and the stop signal** for each bet before it starts [L2013].
6. **Protect wandering.** Name a few hours a week, or one project, with no fixed destination [L2018].
7. **Remove a gatekeeper.** Find one approval step that only says *no* to unproven ideas and replace it with a
   cheap test [L2011].
8. **Harvest every failure.** Write what you learned and who takes it into the next bet [L2018].

Start-ups: before step 4, list the three biggest risks to the company and fund experiments that remove them,
one at a time [AOA01].

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A 25-person e-commerce tooling company has eight experiments running. Six are small feature tests; two are big:
a marketplace for templates (odds maybe 15%, payoff possibly 5x current revenue) and a move into enterprise
sales. They set a failure budget of 15% of engineering time for the year. Stop signals are written down: the
template marketplace stops if fewer than 200 sellers list within 90 days. It gets 140; they stop it, but two of
its engineers carry the seller-onboarding code into the enterprise product, which ships three months earlier
than planned (all invented).

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Bets without stop signals.** Without them, *experiments* become permanent projects.
- **Copying the scale.** *Multibillion-dollar* is right for his company, not yours. Scale the budget to what
  you can lose and survive.
- **Treating every idea as a long-tail bet.** The 10% at 100x logic only works if the payoff can really be that
  big. Most ideas aren't.
- **Survivorship.** His examples are bets that worked, or failures that fed one that did. Many companies took
  similar bets and didn't survive them.
- **Limits:** the 10% and 100x figures are an illustration in his letter, not a measured hit rate.
