# 10 · The flywheel and scale economics

> "Focus on cost improvement makes it possible for us to afford to lower prices, which drives growth. Growth
> spreads fixed costs across more sales, reducing cost per unit, which makes possible more price reductions."
> [L2001]

## What he said

**A virtuous cycle (1999).** In the 1999 letter, each new product makes Amazon relevant to more customers and
brings them back more often: "as we expand our offering, we create a virtuous cycle for the whole business."
[L1999] On operations, he linked cost and experience: "Often, the best way to drive one of these is to deliver
the other." [L1999] Faster distribution means fewer customer contacts per order, lower service costs and a
better experience (our paraphrase [L1999]).

**A scale business (2000).** "Online selling (relative to traditional retailing) is a scale business
characterized by high fixed costs and relatively low variable costs." [L2000] That, he wrote, "makes it difficult to be a
medium-sized e-commerce company." [L2000] This was his explanation for the failure of two start-ups Amazon had
invested in. He also gave up a metaphor that had guided him since 1994, the Internet as a land rush; its
usefulness, he wrote, "largely faded away" [L2000].

**The loop (2001).** The 2001 version is the quote at the top: lower costs pay for lower prices, lower prices
drive growth, growth spreads fixed costs, and the loop repeats. "Please expect us to repeat this loop."
[L2001]

**Customer experience as a fixed cost (2002, 2003).** Selection, product information, recommendations and
software features are mostly fixed costs, more like publishing than retail, so their cost per sale falls as
sales grow (our paraphrase [L2002]). Defects work the other way: "Eliminating defects improves costs and leads
to better customer experience." [L2002] In 2003 he named it: "relentlessly driving the 'price-cost structure
loop' will leave us with a stronger, more valuable business." [L2003] His summary in 2002: "what's good for
customers is good for shareholders." [L2002]

**Price cuts against the math (2005).** Amazon's elasticity data could predict what a price cut would do this
quarter, and "the volume increase in the short term is never enough to pay for the price decrease." [L2005]
Amazon cut prices anyway, as a judgment about five or ten years (our paraphrase [L2005]; chapter 05).

**The flywheel, named (2014, 2015).** Marketplace let third-party sellers compete on Amazon's own product
pages. "The success of this hybrid model accelerated the Amazon flywheel." [L2014] More selection drew more
customers, which drew more sellers, and the scale fed lower prices (our paraphrase [L2014]). Fulfillment by
Amazon linked the pieces: "FBA completes the circle: Marketplace pumps energy into Prime, and Prime pumps energy
into Marketplace." [L2014] His comment on why this works: "Maintaining a firm grasp of the obvious is more
difficult than one would think it should be." [L2014] Sellers want more sales; Prime eligibility gives them
more sales (our paraphrase [L2014]). By 2015 he described Prime Video as feeding "the Prime flywheel" [L2015].

**Losses on a fixed-cost business (2018).** Asked about the years of losses: "It's a fixed-cost business." [ECW18]
Internal metrics showed that above a certain volume the company would cover its fixed costs and be profitable
(our paraphrase [ECW18]).

## How to apply it: the loop map (our suggestion)

1. **Draw your loop.** Write four to six boxes: what customers get (price, selection, speed), what that does to
   volume, what volume does to cost per unit, and how lower cost feeds back to customers [L2001].
2. **Mark fixed and variable.** For each cost that drives customer experience, mark it fixed (software,
   content, a catalogue) or variable (shipping, support per order). Fixed costs shrink per sale as you grow
   [L2002].
3. **Find the defect line.** Which defects raise your costs *and* hurt customers? Fixing those turns the loop
   from both sides [L2002, L2001].
4. **Name the second side.** If you have two customer groups (buyers and sellers, riders and drivers), write
   what each gives the other. Find the program that links them, the way FBA linked Marketplace and Prime
   [L2014].
5. **Check the scale threshold.** Estimate the volume at which fixed costs are covered. If you can't reach it
   with the money you have, you're the medium-sized company of 2000 [L2000, ECW18].
6. **Decide what you pass back.** When costs fall, how much goes to customers in price or experience, and how
   much to margin? Write the rule down; it's a judgment, not a calculation [L2005].
7. **Ask the obvious question.** What do your customers, or your sellers, obviously want? Is the loop giving
   it to them? [L2014]

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A specialty-coffee subscription company maps its loop: lower price per bag brings more subscribers, more
volume gets better green-coffee contracts and fuller roaster runs, lower cost per bag pays for lower prices.
Fixed costs: the roaster, the website, the sourcing trips. Variable: shipping and packaging. The defect line is
*bags arriving stale*, which costs a free replacement and a likely cancellation. The scale threshold is 9,000
subscribers to keep the roaster full (invented); they have 5,200. They decide to pass half of each cost saving
back to subscribers and add a second side: small farms that sell direct, for a fee, through their store.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **A loop on paper only.** If you can't measure each arrow, it's a diagram, not a flywheel.
- **Variable-cost businesses.** If most of your customer-experience cost grows with each order, scale helps
  less than the loop suggests.
- **Passing nothing back.** If every saving goes to margin, the loop stops turning.
- **Medium-sized trap.** In a scale business, being in the middle is the hardest place [L2000].
- **Limits:** these letters describe one company's economics over twenty years. *Flywheel* appears in his
  letters only from 2014; the earlier versions are called a cycle or a loop.
