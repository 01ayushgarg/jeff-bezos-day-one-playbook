# 04 · Free cash flow, inputs and outputs

> "When forced to choose between optimizing the appearance of our GAAP accounting and maximizing the present
> value of future cash flows, we'll take the cash flows." [L1997]

## What he said

**Cash flow over accounting earnings (1997, 2001, 2004).** The 1997 principle above came back in later letters
as the reason for Amazon's financial focus. In 2001 he explained why: "a share of stock is a share of a
company's future cash flows" [L2001]. If you knew a company's future cash flows and its future share count, you
would know roughly what a share is worth (our paraphrase [L2001]). By 2004 the measure had a name: "Our
ultimate financial measure, and the one we most want to drive over the long-term, is free cash flow per
share." [L2004]

**Growth can destroy value (2004).** The 2004 letter works through an invented business: a machine that
transports people, bought for $160 million, with a 10% net margin. Adding machines doubles earnings every
year, and over four years the business shows $150 million of cumulative earnings and $530 million of
cumulative negative free cash flow (his figures, our summary [L2004]). His conclusion: "Paradoxically, from a
cash flow perspective, the slower this business grows the better off it is." [L2004] And on a popular
shortcut: "EBITDA isn't cash flow." [L2004]

**Share count matters too (2001, 2004).** Cash flow *per share* means dilution counts. In 2001 Amazon targeted
average net dilution from employee options of 3% a year for five years (his figure [L2001]); in 2004 he
reported total shares plus awards flat on the year (our paraphrase [L2004]).

**Manage the inputs, not the outputs (2009, 2013).** New senior leaders, he wrote, are often surprised how little
time Amazon spends discussing financial results (our paraphrase [L2009]). The reason: "focusing our energy on
the controllable inputs to our business is the most effective way to maximize financial outputs over time."
[L2009] His count for 2010: 452 detailed goals with owners and dates; 360 of them directly about customer
experience; *free cash flow* mentioned four times; *net income* and *operating profit* not at all (his
figures, our summary [L2009]). In 2013 he called streaming growth figures "output metrics" that suggested Amazon
was "focusing on the right inputs." [L2013]

**The stock is not the company (2000, 2012, 2018, 2020).** In the 2000 letter, with the shares down more than
80%, he cited Benjamin Graham's line that the market votes in the short term and weighs in the long term, and
added: "We're a company that wants to be weighed" [L2000]. In 2012: "We aren't 10% smarter when that happens
and conversely aren't 10% dumber when the stock goes the other way." [L2012] In 2018, recalling the crash: "the
stock is not the company and the company is not the stock." [ECW18] Every internal metric, he said, was
improving while the stock fell (our paraphrase [ECW18]). And in 2020: stock prices "are a prediction of future
cash flows discounted back to the present." [L2020]

**Growth versus profit: the view changed.** In 1997: "At this stage, we choose to prioritize growth because we
believe that scale is central to achieving the potential of our business model." [L1997] In 2001, after the
crash, he described four years of focus on growth, then almost two years on cutting costs, and only then a
balance of the two (our paraphrase [L2001]). By 2004 the headline measure was free cash flow per share
[L2004]. **Our reading:** growth first was a bet that scale would pay; the cash-flow lens is how he later
judged whether it did.

## How to apply it: the input tree and the cash test (our suggestion)

1. **Pick one output.** Free cash flow per share if you're profitable or close; otherwise cash burn per unit of
   customer value (our suggestion). Write it at the top of a page.
2. **List the controllable inputs** that drive it: selection, price, speed, defects, cost per unit, retention.
   Inputs are things a team can change this week.
3. **Set goals on inputs.** Each goal gets an owner, a deliverable and a date [L2009].
4. **Count your goal language.** In your goals list, count how many mention revenue, profit or margin and how
   many mention a customer-facing input. **Our suggestion:** if outputs dominate, rewrite them as inputs.
5. **Run the machine test on growth.** For any growth plan that needs capital, put the earnings next to the
   free cash flow for each year. If earnings grow while cumulative cash flow sinks, model whether growing
   slower is better [L2004].
6. **Check dilution.** Estimate the share count in three years from your option plan and fundraising, and use
   per-share numbers [L2001].
7. **Write the stock rule.** One line for the team on what you'll say when the valuation moves 30% in a month
   [L2012, ECW18].

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A subscription box company reports 80% revenue growth. Its plan is to open three warehouses next year. The
team writes earnings and free cash flow side by side: earnings rise from $1.2M to $2.5M, but warehouse spending
turns free cash flow from +$0.4M to -$3.1M, and the warehouses only fill to 60% by year three (all invented).
Slower growth, one warehouse at a time, filled before the next opens, gives lower earnings and positive
cumulative cash flow. Their goals list had 31 goals; 19 mentioned revenue. They rewrite 12 of those as inputs:
on-time shipping, box defects per thousand, and first-month cancellations.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Inputs nobody can control.** *Increase customer happiness* is an output in disguise. An input is something
  a team can move directly.
- **Inputs that drift into proxies.** An input metric chosen years ago can stop measuring what matters
  (chapter 08).
- **Ignoring outputs completely.** He says Amazon takes financial results seriously [L2009]. Inputs are where
  the effort goes, not a reason to stop reading the cash flow statement.
- **Limits:** the cash-flow arguments are written for public-company shareholders. For an early start-up, the
  cash test still applies; per-share precision mostly doesn't until you raise.
