# 12 · Regret minimization and personal decisions

> "I knew that if I failed I wouldn't regret that, but I knew the one thing I might regret is not ever having
> tried." [AOA01]

## What he said

**The 1994 decision (told in 2001).** He was at a Wall Street firm when he decided to start an online
bookstore. His boss took him on a two-hour walk in Central Park and told him it sounded like a good idea, but a
better one for someone who didn't already have a good job; he asked him to think for 48 hours (our paraphrase
[AOA01]). The way he decided, in his words, was "what I called — which only a nerd would call — a 'regret
minimization framework.'" [AOA01] He projected himself to age 80, looking back, and asked which choice would
leave fewer regrets (our paraphrase [AOA01]). Framed that way, it was "an incredibly easy decision." [AOA01]

**Why it works, in his account (2001).** Looking from 80 "gets you away from some of the daily pieces of
confusion." [AOA01] His example was the annual bonus he walked away from by leaving mid-year: the kind of thing
that confuses you in the short term (our paraphrase [AOA01]). And: "Most regrets, by the way, are acts of
omission and not commission." [AOA01]

**Not just for careers (2001).** "That works for career decisions. It works for family decisions." [AOA01] His
example was wanting to watch his young son grow up (our paraphrase [AOA01]).

**The same story in 2018, with a change.** Telling it again to the Economic Club, he called it a decision
"that I made with my heart and not my head." [ECW18] The age moved: "I always project myself forward to age
80, but as I get older I'm starting to do 90." [ECW18] And on regret: "most of our regrets are acts of
omission, they're things we didn't try, it's the path untraveled." [ECW18]

**Be realistic about the odds (2001, 2023).** He told his parents, who invested much of their savings, that he
thought there was "a 70 percent chance that they would lose their whole investment" [AOA01]. Expecting likely
failure, he said, "relieves the pressure of self-doubt." [AOA01] In 2023 he described the same 30% chance of
success, told to early investors, and the mindset it needs: "you have to have this duality in your head"
[LEX405 01:19:37], knowing the odds and still being completely sure it will work (our paraphrase
[LEX405 01:19:37]).

**Passion, not the gold rush (2001).** "Do something you're very passionate about, and don't try to chase what
is kind of the 'hot passion' of the day." [AOA01] His example was 1999, when people left work they cared about to
join the Internet rush, as doctors once left medicine to pan for gold in California (our paraphrase [AOA01]).

**Stress means you haven't acted (2001).** "Stress primarily comes from not taking action over something that
you can have some control over." [AOA01] Making the first call or sending the first email, even before the
problem is solved, reduces it (our paraphrase [AOA01]).

**A note on the source.** The Academy of Achievement's page shows a headline version of the key line that says
he *would* regret failing. The interview transcript on the same page says he *wouldn't* regret failing. This
playbook quotes the transcript.

## How to apply it: the regret-minimization exercise (our suggestion)

1. **Write the decision** as two or three concrete options, including *don't do it*.
2. **Do the realistic odds.** For each option, write your honest estimate of success and the worst likely
   outcome. Tell the people affected [AOA01].
3. **Project to 80** (or 90). Write a paragraph, in the first person, from that age about each option [AOA01,
   ECW18].
4. **Separate omission from commission.** For each option, which regret would be about something you didn't
   try? [AOA01]
5. **List the short-term confusions.** The bonus, the title, the awkward conversation. Mark which ones will
   matter at 80 [AOA01].
6. **Check passion versus rush.** Would you do this if nobody else were doing it this year? [AOA01]
7. **Decide, then take the first action within 48 hours.** One call, one email [AOA01].

This is for personal one-way doors. For business decisions, use chapter 05.

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A senior engineer at a large bank is offered the chance to join a friend's two-person start-up in agricultural
robotics. Options: join now, join in a year after the bonus, or stay. Her realistic odds of the start-up
succeeding: maybe 20%. Writing as herself at 80, *join in a year* reads as a polite version of *stay*; *join
now and it failed* reads as a story she'd tell happily. The short-term confusions are the bonus (four months
away) and her title. She asks: would she want to do this if robotics weren't fashionable? Yes; she grew up on a
farm. She resigns the next week and tells her family the 20% figure first (all invented).

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **A license for every leap.** The framework favors trying, but he paired it with realistic odds and money he
  could lose. It doesn't make a reckless option sensible.
- **Ignoring other people.** His wife and parents were part of the decision. Your regrets aren't the only ones.
- **Only imagining success.** Write the 80-year-old's view of the failure case too.
- **Limits:** this is his account of one decision, told after it worked. Survivorship applies. The 2001 and
  2018 tellings agree on the substance but differ in emphasis: in 2001 a framework made the decision easy; in
  2018 it was heart over head, and the age had moved from 80 to 90.
