# 06 · High standards

> "Unrealistic beliefs on scope – often hidden and undiscussed – kill high standards." [L2017]

## What he said

The 2017 letter is his fullest account. He sets up four questions and answers each (our structure; his
answers).

**1. Intrinsic or teachable?** "I believe high standards are teachable." [L2017] People learn them by exposure;
"High standards are contagious." [L2017] So do low ones: bring someone into a low-standards team and they adapt
to that too (our paraphrase [L2017]).

**2. Universal or domain specific?** "I believe high standards are domain specific, and that you have to learn
high standards separately in every arena of interest." [L2017] His own example: when he started Amazon he had
high standards on inventing, customer care and hiring, "But I didn't have high standards on operational
process" [L2017], including keeping fixed problems fixed and removing defects at the root; colleagues taught
him (our paraphrase [L2017]). The lesson: "You can consider yourself a person of high standards in general and
still have debilitating blind spots." [L2017]

**3. What does it take?** Two things: "you have to be able to recognize what good looks like in that domain"
[L2017], and you need realistic expectations for how much work the result takes, which he calls *scope*
[L2017]. His illustration is a friend learning a free-standing handstand, whose coach told her it takes about
six months of daily practice, not two weeks; expecting two weeks leads to quitting (the coach's advice, our
paraphrase [L2017]). Amazon's six-page memos are the business version: when a memo is weak, it's usually
because the writer thought it could be done in a day or two, when a great one might take a week or more
(our paraphrase [L2017]; chapter 11).

**4. Do you need the skill yourself?** Not always: "The football coach doesn't need to be able to throw, and a
film director doesn't need to be able to act." [L2017] Someone on the team needs the skill; the leader needs to
recognize the standard and teach the scope (our paraphrase [L2017]).

**Why bother.** Better products, easier recruiting, and protection for the work nobody sees: "a culture of high
standards is protective of all the 'invisible' but crucial work that goes on in every company." [L2017] And:
"high standards are fun!" [L2017]

His summary of the four elements: "they are teachable, they are domain specific, you must recognize them, and
you must explicitly coach realistic scope." [L2017]

**Fix the root, not the instance (2001, 2018).** In 2001: "Eliminating the root causes of errors saves us money
and saves customers time." [L2001] In 2018 he described what happens when a customer email to him reveals a
defect: he asks the team for "real root causes and then real root fixes" [ECW18], so "you're fixing it for
every customer." [ECW18]

**Big things and paper cuts (2023).** Most energy should go to the big things, but every customer experience
also has small defects: "we call those paper cuts. We make long lists of them." [LEX405 01:38:10] Teams on the
big issues never work down to the small ones, so Amazon gives paper cuts their own dedicated teams (our
paraphrase [LEX405 01:38:10]).

## How to apply it: the standards check (our suggestion)

Run it on one domain at a time: a document type, a product flow, a process, a hire.

1. **Name the domain**, narrowly. *Customer onboarding emails*, not *quality*.
2. **Show what good looks like.** Collect two or three examples you'd call excellent, inside or outside the
   company. If nobody can produce one, that is the first gap [L2017].
3. **Write the scope.** How long does excellent take here? Ask someone who has done it. Write the number down
   and tell the team before the work starts [L2017].
4. **Find the skill.** Who on the team has it? If nobody, hire, borrow or train; the leader doesn't need it but
   must recognize it [L2017].
5. **Blind-spot scan.** List the domains you personally own. Mark any where you couldn't describe excellent.
   Ask a colleague to mark them too [L2017].
6. **Root-cause one defect a week.** Pick one customer complaint, find the causes, and fix them for every
   customer, not just this one [ECW18].
7. **Keep a paper-cut list.** Put small defects on one list, and give someone time that isn't taken by the big
   projects [LEX405 01:38:10].

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A design agency's proposals win 18% of pitches (invented). Running the check on *proposals*: the partners agree
on three past proposals they'd call excellent. The scope question shows the team budgets four hours per
proposal; the excellent ones took about three days. Nobody on the team writes well, so they bring in an editor
for the next six. The blind-spot scan shows the founder has high standards for design and none for pricing
pages. The paper-cut list starts with eleven small annoyances clients mention, from a broken invoice link to a
confusing kickoff email; one junior designer gets Friday afternoons to fix them.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **High standards as harshness.** His version is about recognizing good and setting realistic scope, not
  about criticism.
- **One standard for everything.** Since standards are domain specific, *we have high standards* across the
  board is a sign nobody has checked.
- **Scope cut quietly.** Unspoken expectations about effort are, in his words, what kills standards. Say the
  number out loud.
- **Paper cuts forever.** A paper-cut team that never finishes needs a cap and a review, like any other team.
- **Limits:** the handstand and memo examples are illustrations. The four elements are his; the steps above are
  ours.
