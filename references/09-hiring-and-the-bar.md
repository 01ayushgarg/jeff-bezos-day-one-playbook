# 09 · Hiring and the bar

> "Setting the bar high in our approach to hiring has been, and will continue to be, the single most important
> element of Amazon.com's success." [L1998]

## What he said

**Hiring first (1997, 1998).** The sentence above appears in both the 1997 and 1998 letters. In 1997 he told
candidates that you can work long, hard or smart at Amazon, but "you can't choose two out of three" [L1997].
The 1997 principles also say every employee should think like an owner, which is why pay was weighted to stock
(our paraphrase [L1997]). He restated it in 2018: "our focus is on hiring and retaining versatile and talented
employees who can think like owners." [L2018]

**Three questions (1998).** In hiring meetings, Amazon asked interviewers to consider three questions before
deciding (his questions, our formatting [L1998]):

1. "Will you admire this person?" [L1998] People you admire are usually people you learn from.
2. "Will this person raise the average level of effectiveness of the group they're entering?" [L1998] "We want
   to fight entropy. The bar has to continuously go up." [L1998]
3. "Along what dimension might this person be a superstar?" [L1998] It may have nothing to do with the job; his
   example was a colleague who had won a national spelling bee (our paraphrase [L1998]).

**The bar moved from hiring to standards (2017).** By 2017 he no longer treated hiring as the whole answer. Asked
whether high standards are something you hire for, he said that if they were, "this letter would need to be
mostly about hiring practices, but I don't think so." [L2017] Standards are teachable and contagious (chapter
06). **Our reading:** in 1998, hire people who raise the average; by 2017, also build a team whose standards
new people absorb. You need both.

**Missionaries, not mercenaries (2007, 2018).** "missionaries build better products." [L2007] When Amazon
buys a company, the first thing he tries to judge about its founder: "Is that person a missionary or a
mercenary?" [ECW18] Mercenaries want to flip their stock; missionaries love their product and their customer.
"By the way, the great paradox here is that it's usually the missionaries who make more money." [ECW18]

**What Amazon looked for (2013).** "We seek leaders who can invent, think big, have a bias for action, and
deliver results on behalf of customers." [L2013]

**Culture selects people (2015).** Cultures are stable partly because people self-select: someone energized by
competition picks one culture, someone who loves to invent picks another (our paraphrase [L2015]). "If it's a
distinctive culture, it will fit certain people like a custom-made glove." [L2015]

**Many ways to be smart (2018, 2023).** "There are 1,000 ways to be smart" [LEX405 00:25:25]; when he meets
people, "I'm always looking for the way that they're smart." [LEX405 00:25:25] He said nearly the same in 2018:
"There are a lot of kinds of smart." [ECW18]

**Hire the long-lead roles first (2001).** Driving to Seattle in 1994, he stopped to interview engineering
leaders, "because that was going to be an important long lead time item." [AOA01] That trip found the engineer
he called the most important person in Amazon's early history (our paraphrase [AOA01]).

**What isn't here.** Amazon's *bar raiser* interviewing role is widely described elsewhere but does not
appear in these sources, so it isn't presented as his method.

## How to apply it: the hiring bar (our suggestion)

1. **Before opening the role,** write the one dimension where this hire must be excellent, and the long-lead
   roles you'll need in a year. Hire those first [AOA01].
2. **Rank against the team.** List the current team in this function. Where would the candidate rank? **Our
   suggestion:** if not above the middle, they don't raise the average [L1998].
3. **Debrief with the three questions.** Every interviewer answers all three in writing before discussing
   [L1998]. Collect answers before anyone speaks, junior people first (chapter 08).
4. **Find the superstar dimension.** If nobody can name one, ask more [L1998].
5. **Missionary test.** Ask what they'd build if money didn't matter, and why this customer. Look for love of
   the product and customer, not of the outcome [ECW18].
6. **Describe the culture honestly,** including what it costs (hours, pace, ambiguity). The right people will
   self-select in; others out [L2015, L1997].
7. **Plan the standards, not just the hire.** Who will the new person learn the bar from in their first 90 days?
   [L2017]

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A 30-person climate-software company needs a head of data. The team's best analyst would rank second among
the four finalists; two would rank below the team's middle and are dropped. Of the remaining two, interviewers
answer the three questions in writing. Candidate A: admired by two of four, superstar dimension *explaining
models to customers*, missionary answers about grid decarbonization. Candidate B: stronger technically, but the
only superstar dimension anyone can name is *compensation negotiation*. They hire A, and pair her with the best
analyst for her first quarter.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Bar as gatekeeping for its own sake.** Raising the average is about the team's effectiveness, not
  credentials.
- **Mistaking intensity for missionary.** Long hours aren't the test. Love of the customer and product is.
- **Culture fit as sameness.** The third question is meant to find what's *different* and excellent about a
  person.
- **Limits:** the three questions are from 1998, when Amazon had about 2,100 people. The hours line from 1997
  describes his company then; it is not advice on how hard anyone should work.
