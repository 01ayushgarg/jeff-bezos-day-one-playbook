# 03 · What won't change, and long-term thinking

> "think about the things that are not going to change over 10 years. And those are probably the big things."
> [LEX405 01:36:55]

## What he said

**The pillars that won't change (2008, 2018, 2023).** In the 2008 letter: "It is difficult for us to imagine
that ten years from now, customers will want higher prices, less selection, or slower delivery." [L2008]
Because those needs are durable, the energy spent on them now keeps paying off (our paraphrase [L2008]). He
gave the same three pillars in 2018, when asked about regulation: whatever the rules, "customers are still
going to want low prices, they're still going to want fast delivery, they're still going to want big
selection" [ECW18]. And in 2023, as advice to any entrepreneur: once you've found the stable things, "you can
tell they're worth putting energy into because they're stable in time." [LEX405 01:37:38] He adds that it is
"astonishingly hard to focus even on just the big things." [LEX405 01:38:10]

**Long term, from the first letter (1997).** The first of the 1997 management principles: "We will continue
to focus relentlessly on our customers." [L1997] The second commits to long-term market leadership over
short-term profit or *short-term Wall Street reactions* (our paraphrase [L1997]). The 1998, 1999 and later
letters attach the 1997 letter and ask new shareholders to read its long-term section, in 1998 suggesting
they read it twice (our paraphrase [L1998]).

**Owners, not tenants (2003).** "Long-term thinking is both a requirement and an outcome of true ownership.
Owners are different from tenants." [L2003] His story: a family renting a house nailed their Christmas tree to
the hardwood floor, which no owner would do (our paraphrase [L2003]). He applies it to customer decisions that
cost sales now and build trust later (chapter 02).

**Why it works (2008).** A long horizon, he wrote, lets a company use the skills it has to do things it
otherwise couldn't consider, and supports the failure and iteration that invention needs (our paraphrase
[L2008]). And the competitive point: "Seek instant gratification – or the
elusive promise of it – and chances are you'll find a crowd there ahead of you." [L2008]

**Long term aligns customers and owners (2012).** To critics who called Amazon's spending too generous to
customers: "I think long-term thinking squares the circle." [L2012]

**Work on the quarter three years out (2018).** When people congratulate him on a quarter, he told the
Economic Club, "that quarter was big three years ago" [ECW18]. His senior executives, he said, should be
working two or three years ahead, not on the current quarter (our paraphrase [ECW18]).

**New businesses need years (2006).** A new business that takes off, he estimated, needs something like three
to seven years before it matters to the company's economics; Amazon's culture "does not demand that they be
large on the day that they are born." [L2006]

**A lever for problems, too (2001, 2023).** In 2001 his advice to young people was to "always take a long-term
point of view" [AOA01]. In 2023, talking about a clock built to last 10,000 years: "Long-term thinking is a
giant lever." [LEX405 02:07:56] He adds that most institutions struggle to think past five years (our
paraphrase [LEX405 02:07:56]).

## How to apply it: the stable-needs map (our suggestion)

1. **List your customers' needs.** Ten to twenty, in their words (chapter 02).
2. **Ask the ten-year question of each:** in ten years, will customers want more of this or less? Mark each
   *stable*, *changing* or *unknown*. Be strict: price, speed, choice, reliability and ease are often stable;
   specific features, formats and channels usually aren't.
3. **Check where effort goes.** Estimate the share of the team's time on stable needs. **Our suggestion:**
   if it is under half, ask why.
4. **Pick one stable need to compound.** Set an improvement you can measure every quarter for three years.
5. **Date the payoff.** For each big project, write the quarter in which it should show up in results. If
   every project pays off this year, you have no long-term work [ECW18].
6. **Run the crowd check.** For anything chosen because it pays off fast, ask who else is chasing the same
   quick win [L2008].
7. **Run the owner test.** Name one decision this quarter that a tenant would make and an owner wouldn't
   [L2003].

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A meal-kit company lists 14 customer needs. Stable for ten years: *fresh ingredients*, *delivery on the day
promised*, *fewer minutes of cooking*, *a fair price*. Changing: *recipe styles*, *the app's social features*.
Two thirds of the product team works on social features and new recipe formats. They move half of that effort
to *delivery on the day promised* (a 94% on-time rate, invented) and set a three-year goal of 99.5%. The crowd
check flags their discount-heavy acquisition campaign: every rival runs the same one.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Long term as an excuse.** Long-term thinking doesn't excuse missing this quarter's commitments. His
  letters pair it with measured results every year (chapter 04).
- **Picking the wrong constants.** *Customers will always want our format* is a hope, not a stable need. The
  stable things are usually underlying needs, not your product.
- **Ignoring cash.** A long horizon needs the money to get there. Looking back in 2018 on the 2000 crash, he
  said Amazon already had the money it needed and didn't have to go back to the markets (our paraphrase
  [ECW18]; chapter 04).
- **Limits:** the three retail pillars are his example for Amazon. Yours will differ. The 2023 advice is
  general but given in conversation, not as a worked method.
