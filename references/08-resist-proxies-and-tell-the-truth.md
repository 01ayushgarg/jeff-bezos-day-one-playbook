# 08 · Resist proxies, and tell the truth

> "when the data and the anecdotes disagree, the anecdotes are usually right." [LEX405 01:34:00]

## What he said

**Proxies are a Day 2 habit (2016).** As companies grow, they start managing to proxies, which he calls
dangerous, subtle and "very Day 2" [L2016]. Two examples:

- **Process as proxy.** Good process serves you so you can serve customers. But the process can become the
  goal, and teams stop looking at outcomes; a junior leader defends a bad result by saying the process was
  followed (our paraphrase [L2016]). "The process is not the thing." [L2016] A more experienced leader uses the
  bad result to investigate and improve the process (our paraphrase [L2016]).
- **Surveys as proxy.** Market research and surveys can stand in for real understanding of customers. Good
  inventors study many anecdotes, not only averages, and the product owner must understand the customer and
  have a vision; testing then finds the blind spots (our paraphrase [L2016]). "A remarkable customer experience
  starts with heart, intuition, curiosity, play, guts, taste. You won't find any of it in a survey." [L2016]

**Metrics as proxy (2023).** He called "the skeptical view of proxies" the hardest of the four Day 1 defenses to
understand [LEX405 01:24:36]. Someone invents a metric, say returns per unit sold, for a good reason. Five years
later nobody remembers the reason, the world has shifted, and the metric is managed for its own sake. Of such a
metric: "that metric is not actually customer happiness. It's a proxy for customer happiness." [LEX405 01:26:04]
He is clear this doesn't mean dropping metrics: "And by the way, you do need metrics." [LEX405 01:27:38]

**Anecdotes versus data (2023).** His rule is the line at the top of this chapter. When they disagree, it is
usually not that the data was collected badly but that "you're not measuring the right thing." [LEX405 01:34:00]
His example: Amazon's metrics said phone customers waited under 60 seconds, but customers complained. In a
weekly business review he called the customer service number from the meeting room, and everyone waited in
silence for more than ten minutes (our paraphrase [LEX405 01:34:43, 01:35:40]). The lesson he draws: "you have
to seek truth even when it's uncomfortable" [LEX405 01:35:43].

**Truth-telling needs mechanisms (2023).** Humans are social animals, not truth-seeking ones, so "any high
performing organization has to have mechanisms and a culture that supports truth-telling." [LEX405 01:29:14]
Two of his mechanisms:
- **Speak last.** "in every meeting that I attend, I always speak last." [LEX405 01:30:49] Ideally the most
  junior person goes first, so that the most junior person "can overrule the most senior person if they have
  data." [LEX405 01:30:49]
- **Distrust the happy reading.** With two interpretations of new data, it's "a little dangerous to jump to the
  conclusion that the happy interpretation is right." [LEX405 01:33:06]

**A mechanism, not a reminder (2012).** "We build automated systems that look for occasions when we've provided
a customer experience that isn't up to our standards, and those systems then proactively refund customers."
[L2012] His example is a customer refunded automatically for a poorly streamed film (our paraphrase [L2012]).

**What isn't here.** A line often attributed to him, that good intentions don't work and mechanisms do, does not
appear in these sources, so it isn't quoted. The idea of building systems instead of relying on reminders is
our reading of the 2012 and 2023 passages above.

## How to apply it: the proxy audit (our suggestion)

1. **List the metrics** your leadership reviews weekly.
2. **Write the truth behind each.** One line: *this is a proxy for ___*. If nobody can fill the blank, flag it
   [LEX405 01:26:04].
3. **Date it.** Who chose it, and when? Has the world changed since? [LEX405 01:25:30]
4. **Run an anecdote check.** For the top three metrics, find a direct way to experience what the customer
   experiences: call the line, place the order, read twenty complaints. If the anecdotes disagree with the
   metric, doubt the metric [LEX405 01:34:00].
5. **Process check.** For the last bad outcome, ask whether the process was followed. If yes, fix the process,
   not just the outcome [L2016].
6. **Survey check.** For your next product decision, write what you know about the customer that isn't from a
   survey [L2016].
7. **Fix the meeting.** Senior people speak last; the most junior speaks first [LEX405 01:30:49]. When two
   readings of new data exist, start from the unhappy one [LEX405 01:33:06].
8. **Turn one reminder into a mechanism.** Pick one thing people are told to *remember* to do for customers and
   make it automatic, like the refund system [L2012].

## A worked scenario (fictional)

An online tutoring service reviews *sessions booked per week* and a *satisfaction score* of 4.6. The audit asks
what each is a proxy for: bookings for *students learning*, satisfaction for *students coming back*. Nobody
knows who chose the satisfaction survey or why it's asked after the first session only. The anecdote check (the
founder reads 40 cancellation emails) shows students quitting after week three because tutors change. The
metric was fine; it was measuring the wrong moment. They add *students with the same tutor at week six* and
build an automatic credit when a tutor cancels late, replacing a policy that relied on support staff noticing.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Metric nihilism.** The point is to know what a metric stands for, not to stop measuring.
- **Anecdote cherry-picking.** His rule is to examine the data when anecdotes disagree, not to follow any
  anecdote blindly [LEX405 01:34:00].
- **Speaking last as theatre.** If the senior person's view is obvious anyway, the order of speaking won't help.
  Written memos read before discussion help too (chapter 11).
- **Limits:** the metrics and anecdotes material is from a 2023 interview, not a letter. The four defenses come
  from the 2016 letter; the details of how he applies them come mostly from 2023.
