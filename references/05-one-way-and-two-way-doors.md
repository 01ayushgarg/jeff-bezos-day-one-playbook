# 05 · One-way and two-way doors: decision speed

> "Type 2 decisions can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups." [L2015]

## What he said

**Two kinds of decision (2015).** "Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible –
one-way doors – and these decisions must be made methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and
consultation." [L2015] He calls these Type 1. Most decisions are Type 2: reversible two-way doors, where a
wrong choice can be undone by walking back through (our paraphrase [L2015]). The trap for large companies is
*one-size-fits-all* decision making: using the heavy Type 1 process for Type 2 decisions. The result is
"slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently, and consequently diminished
invention." [L2015] A footnote adds that the opposite mistake also exists, but companies that make it tend not
to survive long enough to get big (our paraphrase [L2015]).

**Four rules for speed (2016).** The 2016 letter turns the idea into practice (our summary of [L2016]):

1. Never use one process for every decision. Reversible ones get a light process.
2. "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had."
   [L2016] Waiting for 90% is usually slow. What matters is getting good at spotting and fixing bad decisions,
   because "being slow is going to be expensive for sure." [L2016]
3. Disagree and commit. If you have conviction without consensus, ask: "Look, I know we disagree on this but
   will you gamble with me on it? Disagree and commit?" [L2016] He says it applies to the boss too, and gives
   an example of committing to a TV show he doubted (our paraphrase [L2016]).
4. Escalate real misalignment early. Without escalation, disputes are settled by whoever has more stamina:
   "'You've worn me down' is an awful decision-making process." [L2016]

**Math or judgment (2005).** Some decisions can be made with data, and he calls those Amazon's favorites; others,
like cutting prices year after year, go against the short-term math and rest on judgment (our paraphrase
[L2005]). "Math-based decisions command wide agreement, whereas judgment-based decisions are rightly debated
and often controversial, at least until put into practice and demonstrated." [L2005] An institution that
won't accept controversy limits itself to the first kind, and so limits its invention (our paraphrase
[L2005]).

**Heart and intuition (2018).** Out loud he went further than the 2005 letter: "When you can make a decision
with analysis, you should do so, but it turns out in life that your most important decisions are always made
with instinct, intuition, taste, heart." [ECW18] On how many decisions a leader should make: "If I make, like,
three good decisions a day, that's enough." [ECW18]

**The 2023 version.** By 2023, describing Blue Origin's aim to become "the world's most decisive company"
[LEX405 00:58:37], he added detail the letters don't have:
- As Amazon's CEO, for one-way doors he often acted as "the chief slow down officer" [LEX405 01:00:06].
- "Two-way door decisions should mostly be made by single individuals or by very small teams deep in the
  organization." [LEX405 01:01:29] One-way doors go to the most senior people, whose job is to slow them down
  (our paraphrase [LEX405 01:01:29]).
- Some technical choices, like a rocket's propellants, "are like quick-drying cement." [LEX405 01:02:14]
- Committing means more than agreeing: "I'm not going to be second guessing it. I'm not going to be sniping
  at it." [LEX405 01:04:35]
- Compromise is a bad way to settle a question of fact, like splitting the difference on a ceiling's height
  instead of measuring it: compromise is "low energy, but it doesn't lead to truth." [LEX405 01:06:00]
- "Most of what slows things down is taking too long to make decisions at all scale levels." [LEX405 01:07:54]

**Our reading of the evolution:** 2005 separates decisions by *how* you can decide (math or judgment); 2015 by
*what happens if you're wrong* (reversible or not); 2016 adds speed rules; 2023 adds *who* decides and how to
settle disagreements. Together they make one procedure.

## How to apply it: the door log (our suggestion)

1. **List every open decision** with an owner.
2. **Mark the door.** Could you undo it within a quarter, at a cost you'd accept? Two-way. If not, one-way.
   Expect most to be two-way [L2015].
3. **Push two-way doors down.** One named person or a small team decides, with a deadline in days, not weeks
   [LEX405 01:01:29].
4. **Stop at about 70%.** Write what you'd need to know to be sure. If you already know most of it, decide
   [L2016].
5. **Slow one-way doors down.** Ask for more ways to analyze them, and take them to the most senior people
   [LEX405 01:00:06].
6. **Facts get measured, not compromised.** If the disagreement is about something you can measure, measure
   it [LEX405 01:06:00].
7. **Disagree and commit, or escalate.** Conviction without consensus: ask for the gamble [L2016]. Real
   misalignment of goals: escalate within a week; don't let stamina decide [L2016].
8. **Set a correction date.** For each two-way door, the date you'll check it and the signal that would send
   you back through.

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A 40-person fintech has 11 open decisions. Nine are two-way doors (the onboarding flow, a pricing test, a CRM
switch, among others) and are handed to named owners with five-day deadlines. Two are one-way: signing a
five-year processing contract, and choosing to hold customer funds directly, which brings a regulatory license.
The CEO slows both down and asks for three more analyses. The head of growth and the CFO disagree on the
pricing test; the CFO says *I disagree and commit*, and the test runs that week. The product and compliance
leads disagree about the onboarding flow because their goals conflict; that is escalated the same day.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Calling a one-way door two-way to move fast.** Ask what it would really cost to reverse: contracts,
  customer trust, data, people.
- **Disagree and commit as a cover for overruling.** He describes it as a real hearing and a sincere commitment
  [L2016]. If the boss always wins, it's just a hierarchy.
- **Escalation as a habit.** It is for real misalignment, not every disagreement.
- **Speed without correction.** The 70% rule depends on spotting bad decisions quickly [L2016]. Without a
  correction date, it is just guessing.
- **Limits:** the 70% figure is his rough guide, not a measured threshold.
